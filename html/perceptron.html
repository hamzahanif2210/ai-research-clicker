You discovered the Perceptron!

A machine that learns

In 1958, Frank Rosenblatt built the Perceptron: a simple model of a neuron that takes weighted inputs, sums them, and fires if the sum crosses a threshold. It was one of the first algorithms that could adjust its own weights from labeled examples — an early, tangible step toward machine learning.

What it could (and couldn't) do

A single perceptron can only learn patterns that are linearly separable — problems where a straight line (or hyperplane) can divide the classes. In 1969, Minsky and Papert showed that a perceptron cannot even learn the XOR function, which cooled enthusiasm for neural networks for over a decade.

It took the idea of stacking many perceptron-like units into layers, and a way to train all of them together, to move past this limitation.

Why it still matters

Every modern neural network, from a two-layer classifier to a trillion-parameter language model, is built from descendants of Rosenblatt's simple unit: weighted sums followed by a nonlinearity. The perceptron is the atom of deep learning.

The Mark I Perceptron

Rosenblatt didn't just publish the idea — he built a machine, the Mark I Perceptron, with a 20x20 grid of photocells acting as a retina, wired to adjustable potentiometers that served as trainable weights. The New York Times reported that the Navy expected it to "walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence."

The long road ahead

Layered networks of perceptron-like units, trained by an algorithm called backpropagation, would eventually deliver on much of that promise — just a few decades later than predicted.
